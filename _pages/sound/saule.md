For some years, I have been interested in the possibility of composing not only
for a space, but *with* it. We tend to think of a venue as the place where music
is played, a more or less neutral environment in which a composition unfolds. Yet
every room has its own acoustic character. It reinforces certain frequencies,
absorbs others, and allows some sounds to linger long after their source has gone
silent.

I wanted to know whether these properties could become part of the composition
itself. Not something to correct or work around, but something to play.

When I was invited to perform at Berghain's Säule, I thought it was the perfect
opportunity to pursue this idea. I would explore the room's acoustic character
and use that understanding to compose a techno live set specifically for the
space. Alongside this, I wanted to bring the [Neurorack](/work/neurorack/), a
neural synthesiser I had developed during my [doctorate](/research/thesis/) at
IRCAM and had recently begun enhancing.

These were two quite different experiments, but they came from a similar
curiosity: what happens when we approach the conditions through which sound is
produced not as fixed constraints, but as material for musical invention?

## An instrument made of architecture

An instrument is never simply a means of producing sound. Its interface,
construction, limitations and particular behaviour influence what can be played
and, inevitably, what we imagine playing on it.

I have spent much of my research trying to understand this relationship,
particularly through the design of new digital and hardware instruments. With
Säule, I wanted to extend the question beyond the instrument itself.

A room is not passive at all. Its dimensions, materials and geometry influence
the way sound propagates and is perceived. Anyone who has spent time on a
dancefloor knows this physically: a bass note that seems to fill your entire body
in one position can feel very different elsewhere. Some sounds linger, others
disappear almost as soon as they are produced.

In sound reinforcement, these effects are usually regarded as problems.
Considerable effort goes into minimising them, making sure that the audience
hears something as consistent as possible across the room. However, I was
interested in the opposite approach.

If a room naturally responds to certain sounds, why not compose with that
response? If a sound continues to resonate after the synthesiser has stopped
producing it, could that decay become part of the rhythm? Could the architecture
introduce something into the music that I had not entirely determined myself?

I did not want to turn Säule into an acoustic curiosity or make a demonstration
of its resonances. I wanted to find a way for these properties to participate in
my techno set, where sound is experienced as much through the body as through the
ear.

## Learning to listen to the room

Before the performance, I was fortunate enough to have access to the empty venue
and its sound system for an acoustic exploration.

I spent time listening to the space, carrying out measurements and observing how
sound behaved in different parts of the room. I was interested in understanding
its particular acoustic character, not as an abstract architectural object, but
as the space in which the music would eventually be heard.

This process required a different kind of listening from the one I usually
practise in the studio. Rather than focusing exclusively on the sound I was
producing, I was paying attention to what happened after it left the speakers.
How did the room respond? What remained of a sound as it travelled through the
space? How did its character change depending on where I stood?

The observations offered a way of approaching the room as an active participant
in the music, with its own tendencies and limitations.

I was not looking for a perfect acoustic model or a way to make every part of the
space behave identically. What interested me was precisely its singularity: the
possibility that a composition could develop a different character through its
encounter with a particular architecture.

I returned to the studio with a new way of thinking about the material I wanted
to write.

## A room that is not a box

Most of what makes the room interesting is also what makes it awkward. Heavy pillars stand inside the room rather than at its
edges, so the low-frequency field is interrupted in the middle of the space
instead of only at the boundaries. The ceiling is not at a constant height, which
smears the vertical behaviour across a range rather than concentrating it where a
rectangular room would put it. And the back of the room is a wall of glass, a
surface that behaves nothing like the concrete around it.

Two further variables sat outside my control, and they behave very differently
from one another.

The first is the audience, and I assumed it would matter less than one might
expect. At these frequencies a wavelength is many metres long and a human body is
a small fraction of it, so a crowd scatters and absorbs far less here than it
does higher up the spectrum. I should be honest that this is a working
assumption rather than a settled fact: published absorption figures for an
audience are mostly measured in seated halls, where much of what is being
measured is the seating rather than the people, and the data thins out badly
below the low-mid bands. A standing crowd on a concrete floor at sub frequencies
is not a well-documented case.

The second is temperature, and that one is not an assumption at all. The speed of
sound in air rises by roughly 0.6 metres per second for every degree Celsius,
about 0.17 per cent, and the frequency of every room mode is directly
proportional to that speed. An empty room measured in the afternoon and the same
room full and warm hours later are therefore not acoustically the same room: a
ten degree rise moves every resonance up by nearly two per cent. For a sharp,
high-Q mode that is a meaningful fraction of its own width. I measured a cold,
empty room and then played a warm, full one, and that difference is the single
largest source of error in the whole approach.

## Writing for something I could not fully control

The question was now how to turn that experience into music.

I did not want to compose an entire piece around an acoustic analysis, or allow
the measurements to determine every musical decision. I wanted them to inform the
composition while leaving room for intuition, experimentation and the unexpected.

I began thinking more carefully about how sound occupies a space, and how the
relationship between presence and absence can shape our perception of it.

Sometimes I wanted the low end to feel immediate, dry and tightly controlled. At
other moments, I wanted to allow the room to participate more actively, letting
sounds accumulate, resonate or extend beyond the gestures that produced them.

The interesting part was not simply finding sounds that would interact with the
architecture, but deciding when to make that interaction perceptible.

A resonance that is constantly excited quickly becomes part of the background.
For the room's contribution to emerge, the music also needs moments of restraint.
Silence, contrast and the distribution of energy became as important as the
sounds themselves.

There was something appealing about composing for an effect I could never
entirely reproduce in my studio.

I could anticipate certain behaviours, but the final result would depend on the
actual room and on the sound system. The composition would only fully exist in
that particular situation.

## Bringing the Neurorack back to the stage

At the same time, I was returning to another project that had occupied several
years of my life.

The Neurorack began during my doctorate at IRCAM, as an attempt to bring neural
audio synthesis into a physical, playable instrument. I was interested in what
happens when a neural network is no longer something we interact with through a
computer interface, but a sound-producing system that can be played, patched and
explored in real time.

I have always been interested in designing instruments as a way of expanding
musical possibilities. An instrument proposes a particular relationship with
sound. It gives access to certain gestures, resists others, and occasionally
leads us somewhere we would not have thought to go.

With the Neurorack, I wanted to explore what a neural synthesiser could offer
within this relationship. In the months leading up to Säule, I spent a
considerable amount of time rebuilding and extending it, exploring new ways of
interacting with its sonic material.

## Two ways of making a sound

The module does not synthesise from an oscillator. The model is conditioned on a
small set of spectral descriptors, loudness, brightness, noisiness, bandwidth and
pitch among them, and reconstructs a waveform from that description rather than
building one up from scratch. Three such descriptions are loaded at once, and a
good part of playing the instrument consists of moving through the space between
them. A morph control traverses that space continuously.

Rendered straight from the instrument rather than taken from the night, this is
that movement on its own: a phrase that begins deep and dark and arrives
somewhere brighter and more metallic, moving one step at a time.

{% include site/player.html src="/assets/media/work/neurorack/audio/neurorack-morph-phrase.mp3" title="Morphing between materials" meta="Neurorack · raw render, one morph step per bar" %}

A second pair of controls lets one description lend only its tail to another, so
a strike can begin as one material and end as something else, which morphing
alone cannot do because it moves every characteristic at the same time.

{% include site/player.html src="/assets/media/work/neurorack/audio/neurorack-hybrid-seam.mp3" title="A repeated strike" meta="Neurorack · raw render" %}

Alongside the neural engine there is now a granular one, reading the same
recordings directly rather than through the model. A single control crossfades
between the two, and the positions in between are genuinely both engines at once.
A texture mode holds a sound open past its natural length, so an instrument built
around impacts can also sustain.

## Inside the set rather than beside it

The part that mattered most for a live set is that it behaves like any other
module in the rack. There are four control-voltage inputs and two gate inputs on
the panel. The first CV is pitch, one volt per octave, and can be quantised to
semitones. The other three can be assigned to any parameter in the instrument,
each with its own attenuverter, offset, response curve and slew, so a single
modulation source can be gentle on one destination and extreme on another. The
gates do more than fire a note: they can freeze the current sound, regenerate it
as a fresh take, step through the loaded descriptions, act as a clock, or hold a
texture open for as long as the gate stays high.

This is what allowed the Neurorack to sit inside the set rather than beside it.
The sequencers, envelopes and modulation sources I was already using could
address it directly, and its sonic character became something to play and
automate rather than something to configure beforehand.

One behaviour took some getting used to. Because the model is conditioned per
note, some controls respond instantly while others can only take effect on the
next trigger. A filter opens under your hand; a change of material waits for the
next hit. I stopped treating that delay as a limitation to work around and began
composing with it, thinking in terms of what could move continuously and what had
to be placed.

## What it is like to play

What interests me most is not the technical complexity of the synthesis itself,
but the way the instrument can be played. I wanted to interact with the character
of a sound, its weight, brightness, noisiness or texture, rather than exclusively
with the technical processes responsible for producing it.

These qualities become something that can be shaped through gestures, modulation
and experimentation. The musician does not simply select a result but develops a
relationship with a system whose possibilities gradually reveal themselves
through playing.

The aim is not to make the technology disappear. It is to develop an interaction
in which the technical processes become part of a musical practice rather than an
obstacle to it.

This balance between discovery and control has been central to the project from
the beginning. I want an instrument that can surprise me, but that I can also
learn, practise and develop an intuition for.

Bringing the Neurorack to Säule was an opportunity to explore this relationship
in a context very different from the laboratory or the studio, where musical
decisions have to happen in the moment and the instrument becomes part of a
larger performance.

## Between two instruments

Looking back, I think the room and the Neurorack were two expressions of the same
artistic preoccupation.

One was a neural instrument I had spent years designing, gradually refining how
it could be controlled and played. The other was an existing architectural space
whose acoustic behaviour I was only beginning to understand.

In both cases, I was interested in making music through something that has its
own particular way of responding.

I did not want the Neurorack to function as a technological demonstration, any
more than I wanted the room measurements to become an exercise in acoustics. I
wanted both to become part of a musical language that could stand on its own.

Building the performance around techno was never really in question. It is the
music I make, and it is what Säule is for. What mattered was that it also happens
to suit the experiment unusually well: its relationship with repetition, physical
intensity and low frequencies gives the room something to respond to. It also allowed me to move between more
direct rhythmic material and passages in which textures, resonances and less
stable forms of sound could take over.

The challenge was to bring these elements together without making the music feel
like a sequence of experiments.

## The night itself

There is a particular uncertainty in preparing a performance for a room that
cannot be reproduced anywhere else.

I had spent weeks composing, testing and adjusting material in anticipation of
how Säule would respond. But from the booth, I could not hear what was happening
across the dancefloor. The acoustic interactions I had composed for were taking
place somewhere beyond my own listening position.

I had to trust the preparation and, ultimately, the music.

Only afterwards, speaking with people who had been standing in different parts of
the room, could I begin to understand how some of those decisions had translated
into the experience of listening.

I find this uncertainty interesting in retrospect. A performance is never
entirely under the control of the person producing it, but this project made that
condition unusually explicit. I had composed with the expectation that the space
would contribute something of its own, while knowing that I would not be able to
hear or direct that contribution in its entirety.

Säule was one of the most demanding projects I have undertaken, both technically
and musically. It brought together years of research, months of instrument
development, and a composition that could only really be tested on the night
itself: even if it can be played on any dancefloor, it would be felt differently
in Säule.

More than anything, it reinforced my interest in making music through systems
whose behaviour can be explored, understood and shaped, but never completely
reduced to intention.
